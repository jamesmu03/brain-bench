The harmonization gap

What we found when we graded every public human intracranial EEG dataset, repaired what we could, and tested whether the repaired data makes better models.

Executive summary

Brain-computer interfaces will be trained on recordings from inside the human brain. There will never be many of those recordings: each one requires a patient who needed brain surgery anyway. The public ones (3,032 people, about 2,300 hours) are the field's shared foundation.

That foundation is cracked. The median public dataset grades a C. Two in three recordings carry broken channels nobody marked. More than half don't say what machine recorded them. We call the distance between data that exists and data a model can use across hospitals the harmonization gap.

Much of the gap can be closed from what is already written down. And closing it makes better models. In a pre-registered test at hospitals the models never saw, data as published detected the first seconds of a seizure barely better than chance (AUROC 0.58 to 0.64). The same recordings, harmonized, reached 0.73 to 0.87. Our first round, on an easier task, fell short of its bar; we show both.

Part I · The scarcity

01Every recording costs a surgery

Scalp EEG is cheap. Anyone can wear a cap. But the signals that let a paralysed person speak through a computer come from electrodes placed on or inside the brain, and those electrodes are almost always placed for a medical reason: usually to find where a patient's seizures begin.

So the supply is fixed by medicine, not by money. Every patient is a scarce event, spread across many hospitals. No company will implant enough people to train a general model on its own patients. The field has to pool.

3,032people in public intracranial datasets
2,289 hof recordings, every dataset openly licensed
77datasets on OpenNeuro and DANDI, all graded

02The harmonization gap

Pooling only works if recordings from different hospitals mean the same thing. They rarely do. Each site uses its own amplifier, its own reference electrode, its own filters, its own names for the same channels and the same events. Most of these choices are never written down in the data.

Definition · the harmonization gap

The distance between brain data that exists and brain data a model can learn from across hospitals. It is not a lack of data. It is a lack of agreement about what the data is.

The gap is invisible until you try to cross it. A model trained at one hospital and tested at another simply gets worse, and nothing tells you why.

Part II · The damage

03Silent defects

We measured the signals themselves in 116 recordings from 59 datasets. The problems are not exotic. They are the kind a careful lab would catch, if anyone had asked it to.

66%have broken or noisy channels that weren't marked bad
51%carry strong electrical noise from the mains
36%were filtered before upload, often without saying so

A model trained on unmarked broken channels learns the breakage.

04Missing context

Then there is what the files don't say. We graded every dataset against 32 checks the field's own standards already ask for. The median grade is a C. Only 3 of 77 earn an A.

57%don't name the system that recorded them
51%don't say what the signals were referenced to
77%say nothing about the patients' condition

None of this is negligence. Funders require sharing, and labs share the minimum. But a grade measures whether someone outside the lab can use the data, and today most of it can't be used without months of detective work. Every grade is public.

Part III · The repair

05It is already written down

The missing details usually exist. The amplifier, the filters, the ethics approval, the patients' diagnosis: someone wrote them down when the study was done. We recover them, each with its source, and keep them marked unconfirmed until the authors agree.

In a first batch of five datasets, this recovered 10 missing details and raised 2 of the 5 grades. The other three were blocked by access.

06Looking past the paper

When the obvious source is silent, the answer is often one step further away. Recordings were acquired as described previously. So we follow the trail.

Across the first 72 datasets, going further recovered 26 more details and changed 7 grades. The wall is access: much of what holds the answer can't be read without a subscription.

07The lab assistant

Repairing data after it's published is slow. Better to fix it before. So we built an assistant that runs inside a lab and turns its messy folder into a standard dataset. It asks the researcher only what it can't work out, and never sees a patient's name.

We tested it on a deliberately messy lab folder built from a public dataset (simulated: synthetic signals, real metadata).

D → Agrade before and after
1message to the researcher
0patient identifiers in the output

It got 11 of 11 dataset details, 72 of 72 event times, 240 of 240 electrode coordinates and 262 of 264 channel names right. One simulated lab is not a real lab. It is a working first version.

Part IV · The test

08A fair experiment

None of the repair matters unless it makes models better. So we asked the question directly, the way a company pooling hospitals would meet it: train on some hospitals, test on one the model has never seen.

Definition · pre-registered

The method, the data, the comparison and what would count as success were written down and committed before any data was downloaded. Nothing can be tuned toward a nicer number afterwards.

The task: tell a seizure apart from the minutes before it, in 281 recordings from 81 patients at five hospitals. The same model, trained three times on three versions of the same data. A, as published. B, with broken channels removed. C, harmonized across hospitals.

Harmonized data won on average, for both models. At the largest hospital, with 57 patients, it added five points, and the interval says that's real. Elsewhere the gain was small or unclear, and at Johns Hopkins, with only seven patients, too uncertain to read.

By the bar we set in advance (a clear gain at two of three hospitals), round one did not pass.

The task was too easy to show much. A seizure is a large signal, and large signals survive differences in equipment. Models scored about as well at new hospitals as at the ones they trained on, which leaves little gap for harmonization to close.

10Disease looks like noise

One result surprised us. Removing broken channels alone (B) slightly hurt. The likely reason is worth naming, because it is the central difficulty of cleaning medical data.

Definition · disease looks like noise

The channels where a seizure begins look, statistically, exactly like broken channels: unusually large, unusually wild. A cleaning rule that can't tell them apart deletes the very signal a model needs.

Round two changed how broken channels are chosen, and removing them still didn't help. So this explanation is not enough on its own. Deciding which channels to drop remains the hardest part of cleaning, and an honest open question.

11Round two: harmonization pays

Round two ran under a second protocol, again written before any of its data was touched. It changed how broken channels are chosen, added a harder task (the first ten seconds of a seizure, where the signal is small), and added one more step of harmonization.

Definition · scale harmonization

Every amplifier, electrode and patient records at its own scale. Scale harmonization puts recordings from different people and hospitals on one common scale, without changing anything within a recording.

On the hard task, data as published barely beats a coin flip at a new hospital. The same data, harmonized, reaches 0.73 to 0.87.

By the bar set in advance, harmonization now passes: on both tasks, with both models, at both hospitals. Signal harmonization alone (C) also passes on the hard task, as round one hinted: it matters where the signal is small.

What it does not show, said plainly. Only two hospitals could serve as tests; a third lost most of its recordings to the new protocol's rules, and one of the two has thirteen patients. Scale harmonization helps within a hospital as much as across hospitals, so it fixes differences between recordings generally, not only between sites. More hospitals come next.

12What we can say

Three things, plainly. Public brain data is in worse shape than its volume suggests, and we can measure exactly how. A large part of the damage can be repaired from what is already written down, with a source for every value. And repaired, harmonized data makes better models at hospitals they have never seen: modestly on an easy task, decisively on a hard one.

The open questions are just as concrete. Which channels to drop is unsolved. Two test hospitals are not enough. The next rounds add more of them.

We will publish every round, including the ones that don't go our way. A claim about data quality is only worth something if the people making it would tell you when they're wrong.
